# Structuring a training plan

## What are the phases of a training plan?
Most plans move through a base phase (building aerobic volume with mostly easy running), a build phase (adding tempo, threshold, and race-specific work), a peak phase (highest specificity and load), and a taper. Phases usually span several weeks each, adjusted to the time before the race.

## What is the purpose of the long run?
The weekly long run builds endurance, fatigue resistance, and fuelling practice. It is commonly 20-30% of weekly volume. Marathon plans often peak at roughly 29-35 km. Half-marathon plans peak lower, and 5K plans lower still.

## How many days per week should I run?
Three to four days suits beginners; four to six suits experienced runners. More frequency spreads load into smaller, more tolerable doses, but only if recovery is respected.

## What does a typical week look like?
A balanced week often contains one long run, one quality session (tempo, threshold, or intervals), and the rest easy running. Quality sessions are separated by easy days.

## How much should weekly mileage build?
Build gradually, with a lighter week every three to four weeks. The target peak depends on goal distance and history, and a runner coming off a layoff should rebuild from current ability rather than past peaks.

## How do I choose a goal pace?
A recent race result or time trial is the most reliable guide. Equivalent-performance calculators estimate longer-race paces from shorter ones, but assume matching endurance training, so predictions for a marathon from a 5K tend to be optimistic without long runs.

## When should I adjust the plan?
Illness, injury, travel, and heat all justify changes. Missing a few days rarely matters; trying to make up lost sessions by doubling up usually adds injury risk.
