# Tapering and race strategy

## What is a taper?
A taper is a planned reduction in training load before a race so accumulated fatigue clears while fitness is retained. Research reviews, including a meta-analysis by Bosquet and colleagues, find that tapers of roughly one to two weeks, with volume reduced substantially, improve performance by a small but meaningful amount on average.

## How long should a taper be?
Typical guidance: 7-10 days for a 5K or 10K, about 10-14 days for a half marathon, and 2-3 weeks for a marathon. Longer tapers risk losing sharpness; shorter ones may leave fatigue. Individual response varies, and experienced high-mileage runners often need a longer taper than newer runners.

## How much should I cut volume?
Evidence favours cutting volume progressively, often by roughly 40-60% in total by race week, while keeping intensity and frequency largely unchanged. A common marathon pattern is to reduce weekly volume by about 20-25% in the first taper week, 40-50% in the second, and 60-70% in race week, relative to peak. These are guidelines, not prescriptions.

## Why keep some intensity during the taper?
Dropping intensity as well as volume tends to leave runners feeling flat. Short, race-pace efforts keep legs sharp without adding much fatigue. Cut the number and length of hard repeats, not the pace itself.

## What is taper "phantom pain" or feeling sluggish?
Many runners feel heavy, restless, or doubtful during a taper. This is common and usually does not mean fitness is lost. Fitness changes slowly over weeks, so a short reduction does not erase it.

## How should I pace a race?
Even or slightly negative splits (second half equal to or slightly faster than the first) are generally the most efficient strategy for most distances. Going out too fast is the most common error, costing disproportionate time late in the race. Start at goal pace or a few seconds slower for the first kilometres, and adjust for heat or hills.

## What should race week look like?
Keep routine, sleep well, avoid new shoes, food, or gear, and do only short easy runs with a few brief race-pace strides. Plan logistics early to reduce stress.
